# Oman's public tender lifecycle: a complete guide for IT bidders

**Oman's government procurement operates under Royal Decree 36/2008, managed by the newly renamed Projects, Tenders and Local Content Authority (PTLC), with all tenders flowing through the Esnad electronic portal.** The system uses a two-envelope approach (technical then commercial), mandates In-Country Value investment of at least 10% of contract value, and imposes escalating Omanization quotas — now targeting **63% for the IT sector** in 2025. For technology companies, the MTCIT's ICT Local Content Stimulation Framework adds a layer of sector-specific requirements that weight local content at **10–30% of evaluation scores** depending on project size. The process remains partially fragmented across multiple portals and entities, creating significant automation opportunities at nearly every lifecycle stage.

---

## The legal architecture: Royal Decree 36/2008 still governs, with a 2025 institutional overhaul

Oman's procurement framework rests on **Royal Decree No. 36/2008** (the Tender Law), effective since October 2008, which replaced the older Sultani Decree 86/1984. The law has been amended by Royal Decrees 120/2011 and 60/2013, with implementing regulations established by Ministerial Decision 29/2010. As of March 2026, this law remains the foundational statute — a fact confirmed by its explicit reference in Royal Decree 57/2025.

The institutional landscape underwent major restructuring in recent years. The **Financial Affairs and Energy Resources Council (FAERC) was abolished** in August 2020 via Royal Decree 108/2020, part of Sultan Haitham bin Tarik's sweeping government reorganization that issued 28 royal decrees simultaneously. FAERC's policy functions transferred to the Council of Ministers and its executive functions to relevant ministries.

The biggest recent change came in **June 2025**, when Royal Decree 57/2025 renamed the Secretariat General of the Tender Board to the **Projects, Tenders and Local Content Authority (PTLC)**. The PTLC has legal personality, financial and administrative independence, and reports directly to the Council of Ministers. It houses four directorates-general: Tendering, Project Follow-up, Procurement of Government, and In-Country Value. The Tender Board itself still exists as a decision-making body; the PTLC serves as its technical and administrative arm.

A critical addition came via **Sultani Decree 59/2024** (effective December 2024), which modernized contract-signing authorities. Contracts below **OMR 1 million** can be signed by a Director-General; those between **OMR 1–3 million** require an Undersecretary or Secretary-General; those above **OMR 3 million** require the head of the government body; and contracts exceeding **OMR 5 million** must undergo mandatory legal review by the Ministry of Justice and Legal Affairs.

One important clarification: **"Etimad" is Saudi Arabia's procurement platform, not Oman's.** Oman's central e-tendering system is called **Esnad (إسناد)**. The term "eSupply" refers to ministry-level supplier registration modules used by individual entities like the Telecommunications Regulatory Authority and Energy Authority — these are complementary to, but separate from, the Esnad tendering platform.

### Procurement methods and thresholds

The Tender Law establishes five procurement methods with defined thresholds:

- **Public Tender (Munaqasa 'Amma)**: The default method, open to all registered suppliers. Mandatory publication in English and Arabic newspapers. Tenders exceeding **OMR 500,000** must also appear in the Official Gazette.
- **Limited/Restricted Tender (Munaqasa Mahduda)**: Invitation-only to pre-qualified bidders, used for specialized work.
- **Direct Award (Isnad Mubashir)**: Permitted for contracts below **OMR 10,000**, emergency situations, monopolized goods, or items with no exact specifications.
- **Mumarasa (Competitive Negotiation)**: Allowed when a single bid is submitted, all bids carry essential reservations, or the lowest bid exceeds market value.
- **Musabakah (Design Competition)**: For studies, drawings, and models. Uniquely, this method requires **no bid bonds, performance bonds, or professional indemnity insurance**.

The value thresholds determine which body handles procurement: below **OMR 10,000** can be procured directly; **OMR 10,000 to approximately OMR 250,000–1,000,000** flows through ministry internal procurement committees; and above **OMR 1 million** must go through the Tender Board/PTLC. All procurement at any level now requires **prior approval from the Ministry of Finance** — originally a pandemic-era expenditure control that has become permanent policy.

## The complete tender lifecycle from a bidder's perspective

### Stage 1: Discovery and monitoring

Government tenders are published across multiple channels. The primary platform is the **Esnad portal** (etendering.tenderboard.gov.om), which as of March 2026 hosts **19,819 registered companies** (10,664 local, 3,934 international, 5,102 SME) and has published **68,875 tenders** cumulatively. The portal currently shows **1,979 active tenders** (1,274 local, 127 international, 578 SME-specific).

Beyond Esnad, tenders appear in English-language newspapers (**Oman Observer**, **Times of Oman**, **Muscat Daily**) and Arabic dailies (**Oman**, **Al Watan**, **Al Shabiba**, **Al Roya**). Sector-specific portals add further fragmentation: PDO uses its own SAP SRM system at srm.pdo.co.om; OQ operates the **Tawreed** portal (tawreed.oq.com); Nama Water Services publishes at nws.nama.om; and the oil and gas sector requires registration through the **Joint Supplier Registration System (JSRS)** at businessgateways.com/jsrs. The Royal Oman Police, Diwan of the Royal Court, Ministry of Defense, and Omantel each run independent tender boards. Third-party aggregators like TendersInfo, BidDetail, and TendersOnTime also cover Oman but should be treated as supplementary to official sources.

### Stage 2: Vendor registration and classification

Registration on the Esnad portal is **mandatory for local companies** before bidding. The process requires a valid Commercial Registration from MOCIIP, Oman Chamber of Commerce membership, tax clearance certificates, and an Omanization certificate. Companies must first ensure their data is current in the MOCIIP system before registering on Esnad.

Suppliers are classified into **five grades** (Fourth through Excellent), based on capital, employees, infrastructure, and experience. Registration fees for a two-year period range from **OMR 100** (Third/Fourth grade) to **OMR 400** (Excellent grade), with **50% discounts for SMEs** on all grades except Excellent. Each tender specifies a minimum required grade, so companies must ensure their classification matches the opportunities they pursue.

International companies not registered in Oman may participate in open/international tenders and must complete registration **within 30 working days** of being notified of an award. While 100% foreign ownership is permitted under the Foreign Capital Investment Law (Royal Decree 50/2019) — and U.S. companies enjoy additional protections under the US-Oman FTA — foreign firms still practically need local representation for government tenders given language requirements, cultural factors, and the advantage local content brings in evaluation scoring.

For the oil and gas sector, an **active JSRS certificate has been mandatory since July 2021** for any company bidding on operator tenders. This is a separate registration process managed by Business Gateways International.

### Stage 3: Accessing tender documents and clarifications

Tender documents are purchased electronically through Esnad or at the Tender Board counter in Al Khuwair. **Fees range from OMR 150 to OMR 1,500** depending on the tender's complexity and value. Online payment is supported.

Once documents are obtained, bidders may submit **written questions** to the procuring entity within a defined timeframe. Responses are shared with **all interested bidders** to maintain fairness, and any modifications trigger formal addenda. Pre-bid meetings and site visits may be arranged at the procuring entity's discretion, with minutes distributed to all document purchasers.

### Stage 4: Bid preparation and the two-envelope system

Oman uses a **two-envelope system** for most tenders. Bidders prepare and submit two separate packages:

**Envelope 1 — Technical Proposal**: Contains the technical methodology, team qualifications and CVs, project approach, relevant experience, compliance documentation, Omanization certificates, and ICV commitments. This is evaluated first. Only companies that meet the minimum technical threshold proceed to the commercial evaluation.

**Envelope 2 — Commercial/Price Proposal**: Contains pricing schedules, cost breakdowns, and the financial bid. This envelope remains sealed until after technical evaluation is complete — and is **never opened** for technically non-conforming bidders.

Both envelopes are now submitted **electronically via Esnad**, though physical submission may still apply for certain tenders. All submissions must include a **bid bond** of minimum **1% of the contract price** (typically 1–3%), in the form of a bank guarantee valid for at least **90 days**. Failure to include the bid bond results in automatic disqualification. The Musabakah (design competition) method is the sole exception to bid bond requirements.

### Stage 5: Bid opening, evaluation, and award

**Bid opening** is transparent — bidders may attend in person or view the proceedings via **live webcast** on the Esnad portal. Technical envelopes are opened first.

The **technical evaluation** is conducted by an evaluation committee formed by the procuring entity, often with consultant support. Criteria include specification compliance, technical approach, team capability, past experience, and — for IT tenders — local content scoring. There is no universal pass mark; each tender sets its own threshold. Only technically qualifying bids advance.

**Commercial envelopes** for qualifying bidders are then opened and evaluated. Oman does not strictly follow a lowest-price-wins approach — the Tender Board applies a **best-value methodology** weighing quality, experience, and cost-effectiveness. The specific technical-to-commercial weight ratio varies by tender, though MTCIT's ICT framework indicates **30% local content weight** for IT projects above OMR 250,000.

**National products from Omani SMEs** receive a **10% price preference**, meaning an Omani SME bidding 10% above a foreign competitor may still win. Award decisions require approval from the Tender Board committee and the Ministry of Finance.

Award notifications are published on the Esnad portal. Unsuccessful bidders can **request written feedback** on their individual tender performance. Notably, Oman has **no formal standstill period** between award and contract signing — a gap compared to international best practice — and the Tender Law contains **no specific appeal or complaint mechanism**. Disputes must be escalated to the **Administrative Court** under Royal Decree 91/99, though the bidder must first attempt resolution with the procuring entity.

### Stage 6: Contract execution

The winning bidder must provide a **performance bond of 5% of the total contract value** within **10 days** of award notification (20 days for foreign bidders). Failure to provide this bond results in the full amount becoming payable as compensation and potential cancellation. Consultancies may substitute **professional indemnity insurance** in lieu of a performance bond.

Contracts follow **FIDIC-based Omani Standard Forms and Conditions**. Variation orders are permitted up to **OMR 100,000 or 10% of the original bid value**, whichever is larger. Payments are disbursed through the Ministry of Finance's e-transfer system with typical retainage of **5–10%** until project completion. Disputes follow a multi-tier resolution path: procuring entity → Oman Commercial Arbitration Centre (established 2018) → courts.

## IT procurement carries additional layers of requirements

### MTCIT's ICT Local Content Stimulation Framework

The Ministry of Transport, Communications and Information Technology (MTCIT), which absorbed the former Information Technology Authority, has implemented a sector-specific framework that varies requirements by project budget:

| Project Budget (OMR) | Local Content Evaluation Weight | Project-Level Omanization | Eligible Company Types |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–25,000 | 10% | Company-level only | Freelancers, startups, micro/small companies |
| 25,001–50,000 | 20% | 40% | Startups, micro/small/medium companies |
| 50,001–250,000 | ~20% | 40%+ | Small/medium companies |
| 250,001–3,000,000 | **30%** | Higher thresholds | Broader categories including large companies |
| 3,000,000+ | 30%+ | Project-specific | All categories including international |

**Bids failing to meet local content requirements are excluded outright** — this is not a preference but a hard gate. For projects above OMR 25,000, large companies must subcontract at least **10% to SMEs** and maintain specific supplier development programs.

### Omanization targets accelerating rapidly

The IT sector's Omanization targets have escalated sharply: from **31% in 2024** to **63% overall and 41% for technical/leadership roles** in 2025. Several specialized IT professions are now **reserved exclusively for Omani nationals**, and visa bans on IT-related expatriate roles have expanded to cover over 200 job categories. Government Circular 2025/2 mandates **complete non-contracting with companies failing to meet Omanization rates**, and the Esnad platform now verifies compliance through direct integration with the Ministry of Labour.

### Data sovereignty and cybersecurity mandates

The **Personal Data Protection Law** (Royal Decree 6/2022, effective February 2023) imposes comprehensive data protection obligations with penalties up to **$1 million and/or one year imprisonment**. Government data must be hosted within Oman's borders, and the TRA requires service providers operating in Oman to house servers locally. **Ministerial Circular 2/2024** on systems integration mandates interoperability standards across government entities. While no formal "cloud first" mandate exists, the government is actively promoting cloud adoption — MTCIT signed a partnership with **Amazon Web Services** in December 2022 to establish joint cloud data service centers, and government cloud (G-Cloud) infrastructure is expanding.

The U.S. State Department confirmed that **Oman has no requirements for companies to turn over source code**, which provides meaningful IP protection for technology vendors.

## Platform landscape: Esnad dominates but fragmentation persists

The **Esnad portal** (etendering.tenderboard.gov.om) is the undisputed central platform, processing **over 95 million page views** and **2.25 million logins** to date. It supports bilingual operation (Arabic/English), PKI authentication, a mobile app on iOS and Android, live bid opening webcasts, electronic document purchase and submission, and integration with government verification systems. A mandatory **tender preparation plan** requirement takes effect **March 15, 2026** — no tender can be published without an approved plan in the system.

The **Takamul (تكامل) National Procurement and Project Management System** is currently under development as a next-generation replacement/enhancement, with pilot deployment expected for 10 entities by Q2 2027. This signals the government's commitment to further digitization.

However, the fragmented portal landscape creates genuine difficulty for bidders. PDO alone procures over **$5 billion annually** through its own system. OQ's Tawreed portal, Nama Water's system, JSRS for oil and gas, and independent procurement by the Royal Oman Police, Diwan of the Royal Court, Ministry of Defense, and Omantel mean that **no single platform captures all government opportunities**. Newspaper monitoring in both Arabic and English adds yet another discovery channel.

## Where companies struggle: transparency, delays, and documentation burden

The U.S. International Trade Administration explicitly notes the **"lack of transparency in government tendering processes"** and that **"once bids are submitted, the bid consideration process is often opaque."** Companies consistently report **protracted timelines** from publication to award, with the Ministry of Finance's mandatory prior approval adding additional processing time.

The **~41% tender cancellation rate** revealed by Esnad data (19,568 cancelled versus 27,568 awarded) is striking, suggesting either budget constraints or over-tendering that wastes bidder preparation effort. Payment delays after contract award are frequently reported. The multiple registration requirements across Esnad, MOCIIP, OCCI, JSRS, and sector-specific portals create significant administrative overhead — especially for international companies that must also navigate Omanization verification, ICV documentation, Arabic language requirements, and local presence establishment.

For foreign companies specifically, the Ministry of Finance's **Circular 1/2025** (February 2025) instructed all government companies to award tenders to Omani companies, signaling a tightening of local preference. International firms increasingly find themselves restricted to tenders explicitly opened to international participation, making local partnerships practically essential despite not being legally mandated. The absence of a formal standstill period or tender-specific appeal mechanism means that companies challenging an award decision face the cumbersome and slow route of administrative court proceedings.

## Seven automation opportunities across the tender lifecycle

The combination of process fragmentation, documentation intensity, and the Omani government's own digital transformation trajectory creates compelling opportunities for AI-powered automation:

**1. Tender discovery and intelligent monitoring.** The fragmentation across Esnad, PDO, OQ Tawreed, Nama, JSRS, ministry websites, and newspaper classifieds makes this the single most impactful automation target. An AI agent could continuously scan all sources, normalize tender data across platforms, and deliver unified alerts. The Esnad portal alone has **1,979 active tenders** at any time, and the combination of Arabic/English publication requirements means bilingual NLP capabilities would add substantial value.

**2. Auto-classification and relevance scoring.** With tenders spanning construction, IT, consulting, supplies, and training across five procurement methods and multiple grading levels, an AI system can automatically classify tenders by sector, method, required grade, estimated value, and submission deadline — then score them against a company's registered capabilities, past wins, and strategic priorities.

**3. RFP document analysis and requirement extraction.** Tender documents in Oman frequently run to hundreds of pages in Arabic with English translation, containing embedded compliance requirements across technical specifications, ICV mandates, Omanization thresholds, data localization rules, and SME subcontracting obligations. AI-powered extraction can parse these into structured checklists, flag mandatory versus desirable requirements, and identify unusual or non-standard clauses.

**4. Compliance pre-screening.** Before investing in bid preparation, companies need to verify they meet minimum grade requirements, Omanization ratios, ICV thresholds, financial capacity requirements, and mandatory certification standards. An automated compliance engine mapping company credentials against tender requirements can immediately surface go/no-go decisions and identify gaps that need addressing.

**5. Proposal assembly and quality review.** The two-envelope system with its detailed technical and commercial requirements follows predictable patterns. AI can assist with drafting methodology sections from templates calibrated to winning submissions, assembling team CVs and experience statements, populating ICV commitment plans, calculating local content percentages, and conducting consistency checks between technical narrative and commercial pricing.

**6. Deadline tracking and workflow orchestration.** Managing overlapping timelines for tender document purchase, clarification question submission, pre-bid meeting attendance, bid submission, and bid bond procurement across multiple simultaneous opportunities demands rigorous workflow management. An AI agent can maintain a real-time calendar, trigger preparation milestones, and flag risk when timelines compress.

**7. Post-submission intelligence.** Tracking award announcements across Esnad, newspapers, and entity websites — then cross-referencing outcomes against bid parameters — builds a competitive intelligence database. Over time, this enables pricing optimization and win-rate analysis. PDO's own use of **IBM Maximo with Watson AI** (which reduced 46 procurement steps to 14 and saved 2,300 hours annually) and the Omani startup **SafaQat's AI-powered bid analysis and price forecasting tools** demonstrate that the market is already moving in this direction.

## Conclusion: a market in transition, with the 2026–2027 window proving decisive

Oman's procurement system sits at an inflection point. The **PTLC's establishment in June 2025** consolidated procurement governance, the **mandatory tender preparation plans launching March 2026** create structured data at the planning stage, and the **Takamul system** targeting 2027 deployment promises a next-generation platform. These reforms signal genuine commitment to modernization, but the current reality still involves fragmented portals, opaque evaluation processes, and significant manual overhead.

For IT companies, the expanding requirements — **63% Omanization**, **30% local content evaluation weight** on larger projects, **data localization mandates**, and the Ministry of Finance's tightening local preference stance — make early compliance planning essential. The companies that will thrive are those that treat ICV and Omanization not as burdens but as structural features to build around, while deploying automation to manage the substantial administrative complexity of multi-portal discovery, bilingual document analysis, and multi-dimensional compliance checking. The **$5.5 billion Omani ICT market** growing toward **$9.1 billion by 2030** ensures the prize is worth the effort.
